# Task Dissection: What Exactly Is AI Offloading, and Do We Want That?

> **Goal:** Move from individual encounters to program-level thinking about the educational value of tasks.

Real-time supervision is not enough. Programs should ask what happens to learning when AI changes the structure of a task.

For every task, ask:

1. What is this task teaching?
2. What exactly would the AI offload?
3. For this trainee at this stage, is that offloading helpful or harmful?
4. What new learning opportunity might AI create?
5. How will supervisors still see the trainee's thinking?

# Conclusions

> **Goal:** Return to the opening case/problem and resolve the central educational tension.

A strong AI Teaching Rounds conclusion can follow this sequence:

1. **Do not abandon established educational principles merely because AI is present.**
2. **Make explicit which parts of the work are educationally valuable.**
3. **Require trainees to show their own reasoning even when AI is involved.**
4. **Use AI output as an opportunity for verification, critique, and feedback.**
5. **Allow AI to remove low-value work only when the learning value of the task is protected elsewhere.**
6. **End constructively rather than with hype or alarmism.**

[Write 2–4 concise paragraphs.]

# AI Teaching Rounds Writing Checklist

## Opening and framing

- [ ] Title names an educational/supervisory problem, not merely “AI.”
- [ ] Article opens with a believable GME case.
- [ ] Case includes a specific learner/faculty/program decision.
- [ ] The problem is visible through behavior, not just explained abstractly.
- [ ] Case ends with the question the article will answer.
- [ ] “Why This Matters Now” explains current urgency.
- [ ] GME relevance is explicit.

## Educational reasoning

- [ ] Human-first learning tasks are identified.
- [ ] The article explains **why** each protected task matters educationally.
- [ ] AI augmentation is distinguished from AI substitution.
- [ ] Learner stage is considered.
- [ ] Productive struggle is preserved where educationally important.
- [ ] Faculty can still observe the learner's reasoning.

## Practical framework

- [ ] Framework is short and memorable.
- [ ] Every step corresponds to an actual supervisory action.
- [ ] Questions can realistically be asked during clinical/educational work.
- [ ] Inputs/context are examined.
- [ ] Trainee reasoning is elicited independently of AI output.
- [ ] Verification is explicitly taught.
- [ ] Future-use guidance is provided.
- [ ] Existing frameworks are properly cited/adapted.

## Program-level analysis

- [ ] Article asks what each task is meant to teach.
- [ ] Article specifies exactly what AI offloads.
- [ ] Offloading is considered by learner stage.
- [ ] New learning opportunities created by AI are considered.
- [ ] Supervisory visibility is preserved.

## Practical value

- [ ] 2–4 applied task examples are included.
- [ ] Cross-specialty table is used only if useful.
- [ ] At least one rapid-use box is included.
- [ ] “Try This” section offers small, low-stakes experiments.
- [ ] Resource box contains annotations explaining practical value.

## Safety and integrity

- [ ] AI use is disclosed when relevant.
- [ ] Human responsibility for final decisions/output is clear.
- [ ] PHI/privacy is addressed when applicable.
- [ ] Institution-approved tools/policies are considered.
- [ ] Bias/equity is considered where relevant.
- [ ] Verification/skepticism is explicit.
- [ ] The article does not treat AI detection as definitive proof of AI use.
- [ ] Claims are supported with evidence rather than hype.

## Ending

- [ ] Conclusion returns to the opening tension.
- [ ] The article tells readers what to protect.
- [ ] It tells readers what can be augmented/offloaded.
- [ ] It ends with a concrete educational principle.
- [ ] Tone remains practical, cautious, and actionable.
