\section*{The Case of Two New Fellows}

On the first morning of fellowship orientation, a faculty member asks two new
fellows to work through the same clinical vignette with an institutionally
approved generative artificial intelligence (AI) assistant. Both graduated
from accredited residency programs only weeks earlier. Both are comfortable
with smartphones, electronic health records, and online clinical references.
Yet the exercise immediately reveals that they did not arrive with comparable
AI preparation.

The first fellow recognizes that the model's answer is plausible but asks
which patient information was omitted from the prompt, checks the cited
guideline, compares the output with a second source, and explains why the
model's recommendation may not apply to this patient. During residency, this
fellow had access to an enterprise AI platform, participated in supervised
case exercises, and had been taught when AI use required disclosure.

The second fellow hesitates before opening the tool. Their residency prohibited
most generative AI use on clinical systems and did not provide an approved
model for educational practice. Their experience consists mostly of personal
use of free consumer tools. They can write an effective prompt, but they are
unsure what patient information can safely be entered, how to evaluate a
model's limitations, or how to explain AI-assisted reasoning to a supervisor
or patient.

Neither fellow is irresponsible. Neither training program necessarily made an
unreasonable choice. Yet the fellows now enter the same stage of training with
different opportunities to demonstrate a competency that is becoming relevant
to clinical practice. Prior AI Teaching Rounds articles have emphasized that
programs should make resident reasoning visible, normalize disclosure, and
protect important ``human-first'' cognitive work even when AI is present.\textsuperscript{1}
A related problem now deserves attention: what happens when the opportunity to
learn responsible AI use depends heavily on where a resident trains?

\section*{Why This Matters Now}

Variation in AI preparation begins before residency. A 2025 Association of
American Medical Colleges (AAMC) data snapshot found rapid growth in AI content
in required medical school curricula, but infrastructure lagged behind
curricular interest. Only 30\% of responding schools reported providing
students secure access to an AI agent for teaching and learning, and 47\%
reported an appropriate-use policy.\textsuperscript{2} The AAMC has therefore
made equal access one of its principles for responsible AI in medical
education, explicitly recognizing that institutions themselves have unequal
access to AI tools and resources.\textsuperscript{3}

The same tension is appearing in graduate medical education (GME). In a 2025
single-center survey of GME trainees, 85.5\% reported active AI use, but only
2.9\% reported formal AI instruction.\textsuperscript{4} In a military GME
survey, 84\% of respondents believed formal AI training was needed, whereas
73\% reported that their current curriculum did not contain enough AI
education.\textsuperscript{5} A scoping review of AI in GME similarly described
rapidly expanding applications alongside heterogeneous evidence, evolving
practice, and unresolved educational questions.\textsuperscript{6}

These findings suggest that access and preparation are developing on separate
tracks. A resident may have extensive personal access to consumer models but
little supervised instruction. Another may have access to an enterprise model
with strong privacy protections but few opportunities to use it during
clinical teaching. A third may train in an environment where AI is embedded in
documentation, imaging, or decision-support workflows but receive little
explicit instruction about how those systems work. A fourth may receive a
strong conceptual curriculum while lacking access to any approved model with
which to practice.

The educational goal should not be to ensure that every resident uses the same
commercial product. Models will change too quickly for vendor parity to be a
durable standard. The more defensible goal is \emph{opportunity parity}: every
resident should have a reasonable opportunity to learn, practice, and
demonstrate core AI competencies in a safe environment.

\section*{Access Is Not Binary}

Discussions of AI equity often divide learners into those who ``have access''
and those who do not. For education, this distinction is too crude. Access has
at least four dimensions.

First is \emph{technical access}: can the learner actually use a capable AI
system? Second is \emph{governed access}: is there an institutionally approved
environment with clear privacy, security, and acceptable-use rules? Third is
\emph{instructional access}: are faculty prepared to teach learners how to use,
question, and verify AI? Fourth is \emph{experiential access}: does the learner
have supervised opportunities to apply those skills to authentic tasks?

These dimensions matter because AI competency is not merely the ability to
obtain an answer from a model. Published competency work includes basic AI
knowledge, social and ethical implications, AI-enhanced clinical encounters,
evidence-based evaluation of AI tools, workflow analysis, and practice-based
learning and improvement.\textsuperscript{7} Entrustment frameworks similarly
emphasize that the safe use of AI should be connected to the learner, the task,
the tool, and the degree of supervision rather than treated as a single
all-or-none skill.\textsuperscript{8}

A resident therefore does not become AI-competent simply by purchasing a
premium subscription. Conversely, a program without the newest commercial
model is not necessarily incapable of teaching responsible AI use. What matters
is whether the learning environment enables residents to practice the
competencies that will transfer across changing tools.

\section*{From Access to Competency: The FAIR-AI Framework}

Programs need a practical way to determine whether differences in local
resources are becoming differences in educational opportunity. We propose
FAIR-AI as a program-level framework: \textbf{F}oundational expectations,
\textbf{A}ccess to an approved learning environment, \textbf{I}nstruction
through supervised application, and \textbf{R}eview of demonstrated
competency.

\subsection*{Foundational Expectations}

Programs should define a small set of AI expectations that apply regardless of
specialty or vendor. At minimum, residents should be able to describe important
limitations of AI output, recognize privacy and bias concerns, disclose
relevant AI use, verify clinically meaningful claims, and explain when human
judgment must supersede an AI recommendation. These expectations can then be
extended for specialty-specific tools.

\subsection*{Access to an Approved Learning Environment}

A competency that requires practice cannot be taught entirely by warning
learners about a technology they are not permitted to use. Programs should
provide at least one safe mechanism for supervised experimentation. This may be
an enterprise model, a locally hosted system, a simulation environment with
synthetic cases, or another institutionally approved tool. Equal educational
opportunity does not require unlimited access, and it does not require entering
protected health information into general-purpose models. It does require a
place where residents can practice asking, checking, comparing, and rejecting
AI output.

\subsection*{Instruction Through Supervised Application}

Didactics can establish vocabulary, but applied competency requires repeated
use. Recent resident education work demonstrates that short, interactive
sessions combining instruction with hands-on clinical cases can improve
knowledge of prompting, ethics, and legal considerations.\textsuperscript{9}
Programs can integrate similar practice into existing conferences, simulation,
journal clubs, quality improvement, or case discussions rather than creating
an entirely separate AI curriculum.

\subsection*{Review of Demonstrated Competency}

Finally, programs should assess what residents can \emph{do}, not which model
they personally use. A resident might be asked to identify unsafe prompt
content, critique a hallucinated citation, compare outputs with primary
evidence, explain uncertainty to a patient, or state why an AI recommendation
should be rejected. Model-agnostic assessments reduce the risk that wealth,
personal subscriptions, or familiarity with a specific interface become
proxies for competence.

\section*{BOX 1 \quad FAIR-AI in Under 2 Minutes for Program Leaders}

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=0pt]
  \item[\textbf{F: Foundational expectations}]
  What should every graduating resident know and be able to do with AI,
  regardless of specialty or model?

  \item[\textbf{A: Access}]
  Where can every resident safely practice those skills with an approved AI
  system or simulation?

  \item[\textbf{I: Instruction}]
  Where in the existing curriculum will residents receive supervised,
  hands-on practice rather than exposure alone?

  \item[\textbf{R: Review}]
  How will the program determine whether residents can critically evaluate,
  verify, disclose, and appropriately reject AI output?
\end{description}

\section*{Same Competencies Do Not Require the Same Model}

A predictable objection is that equal AI access is impossible. Institutions
have different budgets, contracts, privacy requirements, clinical systems,
patient populations, and technology strategies. Even within a single
institution, different specialties may use different AI tools. These
differences are real, and attempting to standardize a specific commercial
model across GME would quickly become obsolete.

The solution is to standardize the \emph{learning opportunity}, not the
vendor. The AAMC's equal-access principle recommends attention to both tools
and training, including institutional sponsorship or financial assistance
when appropriate, while also acknowledging differences in resources across
institutions.\textsuperscript{10} For GME, this can be translated into a
minimum educational floor: residents should have access to an approved
environment sufficient to practice core competencies, even if higher-resource
programs provide additional tools.

This distinction also prevents a second inequity. If programs rely on residents
to purchase their own premium subscriptions, learners with greater financial
resources may receive more opportunities to experiment with advanced models.
If programs instead prohibit all model use without providing supervised
alternatives, residents may practice privately, without faculty guidance,
institutional safeguards, or feedback. Neither approach creates a reliable
competency-based learning environment.

\section*{BOX 2 \quad Five Questions for an AI Equity Audit}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item What AI competencies do we expect every resident to demonstrate by
        graduation?
  \item Can every resident access an approved environment in which those
        competencies can be practiced?
  \item Do residents receive comparable supervised practice, or does experience
        depend on rotation, specialty, faculty interest, or personal
        subscriptions?
  \item Are we assessing transferable skills such as verification, disclosure,
        privacy, bias recognition, and judgment rather than fluency with one
        commercial model?
  \item What will we offer learners who enter with substantially less AI
        exposure than their peers?
\end{enumerate}

\section*{The Resident With Only Consumer-Model Experience}

A resident who has used free consumer AI extensively may appear highly
experienced. Yet frequent use can coexist with gaps in privacy awareness,
evidence verification, and clinical governance. The educational response
should not begin by asking how many prompts the resident has written. Instead,
faculty can ask the resident to demonstrate how they would determine whether
patient information may be entered, verify a clinical claim against an
authoritative source, and disclose AI assistance when relevant. Personal
experience can then become an asset rather than a substitute for competency.

\section*{The Resident From an AI-Restricted Program}

A learner with little prior exposure should not be labeled deficient simply
because their previous institution restricted model access. Programs can
provide a low-stakes onboarding exercise using synthetic clinical information:
generate an answer, identify what the model does not know, locate one factual
error or unverifiable claim, and explain how the output should change---or not
change---the clinical plan. The goal is rapid calibration, not remediation for
a technology the learner was never permitted to practice.

\section*{The Resident From an AI-Rich Program}

The opposite problem also matters. Extensive access can produce familiarity
without disciplined skepticism. Benton and colleagues found high AI use among
GME trainees despite very limited formal instruction, suggesting that exposure
should not be assumed to equal readiness.\textsuperscript{4} Residents arriving
from AI-rich environments should therefore demonstrate the same core
competencies as their peers, including the ability to work without AI when the
task requires independent reasoning.

\section*{BOX 3 \quad Try This: Three Small Experiments to Mind the AI Gap}

\textbf{Inventory access before teaching.}
Ask residents anonymously which AI tools they can access, whether those tools
are personally paid or institutionally provided, and whether they know which
uses are approved. Compare the answers with faculty assumptions. The goal is
not surveillance; it is to identify hidden differences in the learning
environment.

\vspace{0.6em}

\textbf{Run one common AI case.}
Give every resident the same synthetic vignette and access to the same approved
AI environment. Ask each resident to identify one useful contribution, one
limitation, one statement requiring verification, and one circumstance in which
they would reject the model's recommendation. This creates a common educational
experience without requiring long-term access to identical commercial tools.

\vspace{0.6em}

\textbf{Assess the skill, not the subscription.}
At the end of the rotation, assess residents with a pre-generated AI output
rather than requiring them to own or access a particular model. Ask them to
critique the output, verify evidence, identify privacy or bias concerns, and
state what they would do next. This allows programs to assess transferable AI
competency independent of personal purchasing power.

\section*{Conclusions}

GME has long accepted that residents train in different hospitals, with
different patient populations, technologies, and local resources. AI does not
require eliminating that variation. It does require recognizing when variation
in the learning environment prevents some residents from practicing skills
that programs increasingly expect them to possess.

The central equity question is therefore not whether every resident has the
same model. It is whether every resident has a meaningful opportunity to learn
what AI can and cannot do, practice with it safely, receive supervision, and
demonstrate transferable judgment. A resident with a premium personal
subscription is not automatically well prepared; a resident from a
resource-constrained or AI-restricted program is not automatically poorly
prepared.

If AI competency becomes an expectation of modern clinical training, access to
the learning experiences required to develop that competency cannot remain an
optional benefit of institutional wealth, local enthusiasm, or personal
purchasing power. Programs can begin modestly: define a common floor, provide a
safe place to practice, teach through supervised cases, and assess the skills
rather than the brand name. The models will continue to change. The obligation
to prepare residents equitably should not.
